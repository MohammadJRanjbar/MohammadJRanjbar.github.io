Persian is hard to hear

Persian brings audio challenges that no existing benchmark captures: a classical poetry tradition built on metre, a traditional music system of its own, and speakers who switch between Persian and English mid-sentence. PARSA-Bench asks whether large audio-language models (LALMs) actually understand Persian when they listen, across speech understanding, paralinguistic analysis, and culturally grounded audio reasoning.

Models know Persian, but struggle to hear it

On most tasks, a model reading the text does better than the same model listening to the audio. The language knowledge is there; the listening is the bottleneck. The strongest open-weight model, Qwen3-Omni-30B, shows the pattern clearly:

Give the model the transcript alongside the audio and weak models climb to near their text-only level. Qwen3-Omni-30B's intent-detection F1 goes from 0.46 with audio alone to 0.72 with the transcript added.

The exception: poetry

On both poetry tasks, audio beats text. Persian script leaves short vowels unwritten, so a line on the page doesn't say which syllables are long and which are short. A recitation does, and that rhythm is exactly what the metre is made of.

Metre detection is still hard. Chance is 0.12, and only the largest model, Qwen3-Omni-30B, gets above it (0.20), the first sign that the task becomes learnable at scale.

Where the audio comes from

  • Real speech from Mozilla Common Voice, for recognition, age, gender and code-switching.
  • Emotional speech from the ShEMO corpus.
  • Poetry recitations collected through Ganjoor, the online library of Persian poetry.
  • Synthesized speech for text-based tasks such as intent, named entities, QA and reading comprehension, voiced with the ParsVoice zero-shot TTS using real speakers as reference voices.
